Over the last 5 years, the maximum drawdown (MDD: the fall from the highest value to the lowest) of major US ETFs varied widely: TLT -50.0%, QQQ -35.5%, SCHD -18.9%. Knowing the size of a possible loss in advance is a key test of whether you could hold a product to the end.

If you know how far something can fall, you can judge for yourself whether you would keep holding it in a downturn. Below are the maximum drawdowns and recovery speeds calculated from Yahoo Finance daily closing prices in dollars over the last 5 years.

How far can my money fall?

Assuming KRW 10 million was invested at the peak, the lowest balance was KRW 5 million for TLT, KRW 6.45 million for QQQ and KRW 8.11 million for SCHD.

🔸 How far each ETF fell from its peak

SCHD (Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF) had a maximum drawdown of -18.9%, so an example KRW 10 million invested at the peak stayed at about KRW 8.11 million. QQQ (Invesco QQQ Trust), by contrast, fell as far as -35.5%, shrinking the balance to KRW 6.45 million.

TLT (iShares 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF), which had the largest drawdown, recorded -50.0%, so KRW 10 million fell to KRW 5 million, or half. This shows that not only stock ETFs but bond ETFs too can fall far below their peak.

All of these ETFs are listed overseas and trade in dollars, so their value in won also moves with the exchange rate.

How long did it take from peak to bottom?

Looking at the length of the fall, SCHD's was the shortest at 262 days, while TLT kept falling for 1,767 days. How much value is lost matters, but how long the fall lasts also weighs heavily on an investor.

SCHD peaked on January 11, 2022 and reached its bottom 262 days later, on September 30, 2022. It had the shortest fall of the three ETFs.

QQQ fell for 366 days, from December 27, 2021 to December 28, 2022, a stretch of about a year in which the balance kept shrinking. TLT fell for 1,767 days, from December 3, 2021 to October 5, 2026, the longest stretch of downward pressure.

How long does it take to get back to the peak?

QQQ took 350 days from its bottom to regain its previous peak price, while TLT has not yet regained its previous peak. Even over the same period, the time each ETF needed to recover differed widely.

QQQ hit its bottom on December 28, 2022 and regained its peak price 350 days later, on December 13, 2023. SCHD needed 656 days, from its bottom on September 30, 2022 to July 17, 2024, to regain its previous peak, a relatively longer recovery.

Compared with their 5-year highs, current prices stand at -5.6% for SCHD, -15.1% for QQQ and -47.9% for TLT. An asset that has fallen around -50.0% needs a gain of +100.0% to get back to its original price, which makes recovery harder.

Can Korean tax-advantaged accounts hold overseas ETFs?

Overseas-listed ETFs cannot be bought directly in Korea's ISA, pension savings or IRP accounts. Keep the account type in mind if, after checking the drawdowns, you plan to buy the overseas-listed QQQ, SCHD or TLT directly.

To use the benefits of tax-advantaged accounts such as ISA or pension savings, one option is a Korea-listed ETF. A Korea-listed ETF that tracks the same index as an overseas-listed ETF can be traded in those accounts. Taxes are covered in the guide comparing taxes on overseas-listed vs Korea-listed ETFs.

Frequently asked questions

🔸 When and how do I report tax on gains from US-listed ETFs?

Gains from trading US-listed ETFs are subject to capital gains tax in Korea. Investors report those gains themselves to the National Tax Service in May of the following year.

🔸 How are dividends from US-listed ETFs taxed?

Dividends from US-listed ETFs reach your account after withholding tax (tax taken out in advance when dividends or interest are paid) is deducted in the US. Tax already paid abroad is then subtracted through the foreign tax credit when your tax is calculated in Korea.

Summary

Looking at maximum drawdown together with the length of the fall and the recovery lets you judge in advance whether you could hold a product while its value shrinks. Rather than going in on returns alone, looking at past losses and how many days the recovery took helps your judgment.

When you check the downside risk of a new ETF or other product, you can look at three things yourself, based on real figures. First, whether the maximum drawdown, the fall from the highest to the lowest value, is within a range you can handle, like SCHD's -18.9% or QQQ's -35.5%. Second, whether the length of the fall to the bottom is something you could sit through, like SCHD's 262 days or TLT's 1,767 days. Third, whether the time to regain the previous peak is manageable, like QQQ's 350 days or SCHD's 656 days.

In one line: checking maximum drawdown and recovery time tells you in advance whether an ETF is one you could hold through a downturn.
