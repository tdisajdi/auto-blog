For a Korean investor using a regular (taxable) account to track the same overseas index, the tax is the same at an annual gain of about KRW 8.33 million, whether the ETF is listed overseas or is a Korea-listed ETF tracking an overseas index. Below KRW 8.33 million, the overseas-listed ETF, which comes with a KRW 2.5 million basic deduction, means less tax; above KRW 8.33 million, the Korea-listed overseas-index ETF, taxed at 15.4%, means less tax.

Overseas-listed ETFs cannot be bought directly in Korea's tax-advantaged accounts - the ISA (Individual Savings Account), pension savings accounts or the IRP (Individual Retirement Pension). The tax benefits of these accounts are available through Korea-listed ETFs that track the same index. Because the tax you actually pay depends on whether you use a regular account or a tax-advantaged one, it helps to understand the details.

Returns and cost structure

SPY (State Street SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust), listed directly in the US, and 360750 (TIGER US S&P500), listed in Korea, both track the US S&P 500 index but differ in their terms. SPY's management fee (the cost the fund manager takes from the assets every year) is 0.09%, while TIGER US S&P500's is set at 0.007%.

Both are affected by the exchange rate. TIGER US S&P500 is listed in Korea, but it invests in overseas assets without currency hedging (a mechanism that keeps returns from swinging with the exchange rate), so it is exposed to changes in the value of the dollar. SPY, being US-listed and traded directly in dollars, also passes exchange-rate moves straight into a Korean investor's returns.

Over the past year, SPY returned 11.39%, while TIGER US S&P500 returned -0.62%. The numbers differ so much even though both track the same index because SPY's return is in dollars and TIGER US S&P500's is in Korean won, so the change in the won/dollar exchange rate over that period is included. The 5-year compound annual growth rate (CAGR - the yearly rate that, applied every year, would produce the total gain) was 10.7% for SPY and 14.8% for TIGER US S&P500.

The maximum drawdown over 5 years (MDD - the fall from the highest value to the lowest) was -24.50% for SPY and -19.15% for TIGER US S&P500. SPY's dividend yield is 1.04%, and its total dividends over the past year grew 4.54% from the year before.

Tax in a regular account, by size of gain

When a Korean resident trades US-listed ETFs in a regular account, a capital gains tax of 22% applies after a deduction of KRW 2.5 million a year. This must be filed by the investor in May of the following year, for example through the National Tax Service's Hometax. Distributions (money an ETF pays out to investors) and dividends have 15% withheld in the US (tax taken out before the payment is made).

By contrast, selling a Korea-listed overseas-index ETF in a regular Korean account triggers a 15.4% dividend income tax on both the trading gain and distributions. Trading gains on Korea-listed domestic equity ETFs are tax-free, but Korea-listed ETFs tracking overseas indices or bonds are taxable. In addition, Korea-listed overseas-index ETFs are taxed on the gain over the holding period when sold, and that gain cannot be offset against losses on other products.

With an annual gain of KRW 2 million in a regular account, the overseas-listed ETF owes KRW 0 thanks to the KRW 2.5 million deduction, while the Korea-listed ETF owes KRW 310,000. With a gain of KRW 5 million, the tax works out to KRW 550,000 for the overseas-listed ETF and KRW 770,000 for the Korea-listed ETF.

At an annual gain of KRW 10 million, however, the overseas-listed ETF owes KRW 1.65 million while the Korea-listed ETF owes less, KRW 1.54 million. At KRW 20 million, the tax is KRW 3.85 million for the overseas-listed ETF and KRW 3.08 million for the Korea-listed ETF.

Tax-advantaged accounts and their limits

The ISA has an annual contribution limit of KRW 20 million and a minimum holding period of 3 years. Gains up to KRW 2 million (KRW 4 million for the low-income type) are tax-free, and anything above that is taxed separately at 9.9%. A reform of the ISA, aimed at taking effect in 2027, is under review in the National Assembly.

Pension savings and IRP accounts defer tax until withdrawal, and pensions taken after age 55 are taxed at a low 3.3–5.5%. Assuming the 5.5% rate for a pension taken after age 55, an annual gain of KRW 2 million means KRW 0 of tax in a general-type ISA (held 3 years) and KRW 110,000 in pension savings.

With a gain of KRW 5 million, the general-type ISA owes KRW 300,000 and pension savings KRW 280,000. At KRW 10 million, the ISA owes KRW 790,000 and pension savings KRW 550,000. At KRW 20 million, the ISA tax works out to KRW 1.78 million and the pension savings tax to KRW 1.1 million.

However, if financial income such as interest and dividends received in regular accounts exceeds KRW 20 million in a year, it becomes subject to Korea's comprehensive financial income taxation. Gains inside ISA and pension accounts are not counted toward this total. The amount above KRW 20 million is taxed together with other income, so if a large gain on a Korea-listed overseas-index ETF is counted as dividend income, the total tax can rise. Dividends from overseas ETFs are also counted toward this total.

What to check for yourself

The figures assume the gain came only from that ETF during the year, with distributions, exchange-rate changes and trading costs excluded. The KRW 2.5 million annual deduction for overseas-listed ETFs applies to the combined gains and losses on all overseas stocks, and the pension savings tax assumes a pension taken after age 55 (5.5%). Early withdrawal from pension savings is taxed at 16.5%.

Between regular accounts, the tax break-even point is an annual gain of about KRW 8.33 million. Below KRW 8.33 million, the overseas-listed ETF, with its KRW 2.5 million annual deduction (capital gains tax of 22%), means less tax. Above KRW 8.33 million, the Korea-listed overseas-index ETF, taxed at 15.4%, means less tax.

Trading gains on Korea-listed overseas-index ETFs are classified as dividend income. If, together with other interest and dividend income, they exceed KRW 20 million in a year, comprehensive financial income taxation applies and the tax base can rise, so the year's total financial income is worth checking as well.

Frequently asked questions

🔸 If I sell both US-listed and Korea-listed ETFs, can losses be combined?

Korea-listed overseas-index ETFs are taxed on the gain over the holding period when sold, and losses on other products cannot be offset against it. US-listed ETFs, on the other hand, combine gains and losses across all overseas stocks, deduct KRW 2.5 million a year, and then calculate capital gains tax.

Summary

When investing in overseas assets from Korea, the tax works differently depending on the account type and the expected annual gain. It is worth weighing the difference between overseas-listed ETFs, which cannot go into tax-advantaged accounts, and Korea-listed ETFs, which can be held in ISA and pension accounts.

There are three things to check for yourself. First, in a regular account, whether your annual trading gain is likely to exceed the KRW 8.33 million break-even point. Second, whether your total annual financial income could exceed KRW 20 million and fall under comprehensive financial income taxation. Third, whether you can meet the holding and withdrawal conditions of an ISA (minimum 3 years) or pension savings (3.3–5.5% when taken as a pension after age 55).

In one line: An annual gain of KRW 8.33 million is the dividing line; the tax difference comes down to how each account is taxed and how much you expect to gain.
