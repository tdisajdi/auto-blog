Invest KRW 10 million at a 5% annual return before fees, and two ETFs whose total fees differ by just 0.1%p (percentage point: the difference between two percentages) end up about KRW 160,000 apart after 10 years. That is about 1.0% of the final balance, and after 20 years the gap widens to about KRW 530,000, or about 2.0% of the final balance.

Total fees are not something you pay from your account each month; they come out of the ETF's assets a little every day. You never transfer cash for them - they are already reflected in the return (the price). Real product data published as of November 19, 2026 shows concretely how a fee difference affects an account.

Real ETF fees and the gap after 10 years

Among the main products tracking the US S&P 500 index, the management fee (the cost the fund manager takes from the assets every year) clearly differs between overseas-listed and Korea-listed products. SPY, listed in the US, charges a management fee of 0.09%, and VOO, another US-listed ETF, charges 0.03%. The Korea-listed 360750 (TIGER US S&P500) charges 0.007%.

Beyond cost, they also differ in maximum drawdown over 5 years (MDD: the fall from the highest value to the lowest). SPY's 5-year maximum drawdown was -24.50%, and VOO's was -24.52%. Both US-listed ETFs trade in dollars, so the exchange rate affects them. The Korea-listed 360750 also invests in overseas assets without currency hedging (a mechanism that keeps returns from swinging with the exchange rate), so it is just as exposed to the exchange rate, yet its 5-year maximum drawdown came to -19.15%. The drawdowns differ for the same index because 360750 is measured in Korean won while SPY and VOO are measured in dollars, so the change in the exchange rate over that period is included.

🔸 What real fee gaps add up to over 10 and 20 years

Assuming the same KRW 10 million grows at 5% a year before fees, the money gap created by real management fees depends on which two products you compare. VOO (total fee 0.03%) and 360750 (total fee 0.007%) differ by 0.023%p. Under this assumption, VOO's balance after 10 years is KRW 40,000 lower than 360750's, and KRW 120,000 lower after 20 years.

Comparing SPY (total fee 0.09%) with 360750 (total fee 0.007%), the fee gap widens to 0.083%p. Because of it, SPY's balance after 10 years is KRW 130,000 lower than 360750's, and after 20 years the gap grows to KRW 440,000.

Why the gap grows over time

The money gap from a fee difference does not grow in a straight line; it speeds up over time because of compounding. That is why the same 0.1%p fee difference produces a gap of about KRW 160,000 over 10 years but about KRW 530,000 after 20 years - more than 3 times as much.

The amount invested also widens the cost gap. Because the management fee is deducted every day as a fixed share of the assets, a larger principal means a larger absolute difference in what is taken each day. The longer you plan to invest, the more it is worth weighing how a tiny fee difference today affects your future balance.

Where an ETF is listed, and which accounts can hold it

Which Korean tax-advantaged accounts you can use depends on where the ETF is listed. ETFs listed overseas (in the US, for example) cannot be bought directly in an ISA (Individual Savings Account), a pension savings account or an IRP. If you want both the index exposure that overseas-listed products offer and the tax benefits of these accounts, one option is a Korea-listed ETF tracking the same index inside the account.

Dividends are paid and taxed differently as well. Dividends from overseas-listed ETFs go through withholding, where tax is taken out locally before the payment. The detailed tax rules and differences are covered in the guide comparing taxes on overseas-listed and Korea-listed ETFs.

Frequently asked questions

🔸 If total fees differ by 0.1%, do I have to transfer 0.1% in cash from my account every month?

No. Total fees are deducted from the ETF's own assets a little every day and are built into the net asset value automatically. You never get a bill or make a transfer; the cost is already reflected in the ETF's daily price and return.

🔸 Can overseas-listed and Korea-listed ETFs be bought the same way in an ISA?

ETFs listed overseas (in the US, for example) cannot be bought directly in an ISA, pension savings account or IRP; the tax benefits of these accounts are available through Korea-listed ETFs that track the same index.

Summary

A tiny difference in total fees is hard to notice over a short period, but through compounding and time it slowly widens the gap between account balances. When planning a long-term allocation, it helps to look not only at asset classes but also at exactly how much each product takes in fees.

When looking at a new ETF or a comparison, you can check the numbers yourself against these points. First, see how much management fees differ between products tracking the same index - 0.083%p or 0.023%p, for example. Second, even with the same underlying index, check whether maximum drawdowns over the past 5 years differ, as with 360750 (-19.15%), SPY (-24.50%) and VOO (-24.52%). Third, check that the product is listed where it can be traded directly in the account you plan to use, such as a pension savings account or an ISA.

In one line: A 0.1%p gap in ETF total fees widens through compounding when you invest for the long term.
