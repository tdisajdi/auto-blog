The yield trap: an illusion created by price moves

Dividend yield is the dividend paid relative to the current share price, so when the price falls, the yield on paper rises even if the dividend stays the same. SCHD returned 33.82% over the past year, while its total dividends over the same period grew by 2.03%. This shows that the dividend itself grew relatively little compared with the rise in price.

🔸 The gap between price gains and dividend growth

On the other hand, an ETF with a relatively low yield can show stronger dividend growth. DGRO's yield at today's price is 1.86%, the lowest of the three, but its total dividends over the past year grew by 8.10%, alongside a 1-year return of 18.77%.

VYM had a yield at today's price of 2.25%, 1-year dividend growth of 4.49% and a 1-year return of 15.38%. Because dividend yield is directly affected by price moves, seeing how much the dividends a company or ETF actually pays are growing requires looking at the dividend growth rate separately.

Cash flow over time: dividend growth and yield on cost

For a long-term investor, what matters is less today's yield than yield on cost: the dividend received relative to the money originally invested. Had you bought SCHD at its price on January 4, 2021, its 5-year average dividend growth of +9.1% would have made its 2025 dividend equal to 4.97% of the purchase price. With the purchase price fixed at the start, a dividend that grows every year keeps lifting the yield on cost.

🔸 How the starting yield and dividend growth work together

A higher dividend growth rate, however, does not guarantee a higher yield on cost. DGRO's 5-year average dividend growth of +7.1% was higher than VYM's +3.8%, but for a 2021 purchase, the 2025 yield on cost was 3.87% for VYM and 3.28% for DGRO, so VYM stayed higher.

This is because of the gap in starting yields. At the start, VYM yielded 3.21% and DGRO 2.33%, and over 5 years the difference in growth rates alone was not enough to close that gap. In other words, long-term cash flow is not decided by the dividend growth rate alone; the starting yield matters too.

Volatility and dividends: looking at maximum drawdown (MDD) too

To keep collecting dividends and stay invested for the long term, you need to be able to sit through volatility when prices fall. DGRO's 5-year maximum drawdown (MDD: the percentage fall from the highest value to the lowest value) was -19.31%, the largest price fall of the ETFs examined.

🔸 Comparing how they held up in falling markets

Over the same period, VYM's 5-year maximum drawdown was -15.85%, the smallest fall, and SCHD's was -16.84%. Bigger swings raise the risk of giving up on a long-term investment out of anxiety during a downturn, so it helps to look at how far the price fell alongside how the dividend has grown.

The three ETFs' 5-year maximum drawdowns ranged from VYM's -15.85% to DGRO's -19.31%, and the ranking by dividend growth was not the same as the ranking by drawdown.

Frequently asked questions

🔸 Can a high dividend yield mean the share price has fallen a lot?

Yes. Dividend yield moves inversely with the share price, so even with no change in the dividend paid, a sharp fall in price can temporarily push the yield on paper up. That is why it is worth telling apart whether a higher yield comes from a real increase in dividends or is an illusion created by a falling price.

🔸 Does past dividend growth guarantee future dividend income?

No. Figures such as the 5-year average dividend growth rate or yield on cost are records calculated from past data, and future dividend payments and growth can change with market conditions and company results.

Summary

Choosing an ETF by a high yield alone can mean falling for an illusion created by a falling price, or missing how much the dividend itself could grow. On the other hand, focusing only on the dividend growth rate can leave real cash flow lower for a long time because of a gap in starting yields.

When you look at dividend ETFs or related figures, three checks help. First, whether dividends keep growing, as with SCHD's 5-year average dividend growth of +9.1%. Second, how yield on cost develops from the starting yield and the growth rate together, as in the case of DGRO (yield on cost 3.28%) and VYM (3.87%). Third, whether the 5-year maximum drawdowns of VYM (-15.85%), SCHD (-16.84%) and DGRO (-19.31%) are a level of volatility you could sit through in a falling market.

In one line: today's yield alone does not tell you about future dividends; you see the full picture by looking at dividend growth, the starting yield and maximum drawdown together.
