While the US 10-year Treasury yield rose 0.85%p (percentage points: the difference between two rates) from 4.06% to 4.91%, existing bonds paying lower interest became less attractive, and the price of TLT, a long-term bond ETF, fell -4.7%. When market rates rise, selling of existing bonds that pay relatively low interest increases and their prices fall, and the longer the duration (a measure of how much a bond's price moves when rates move), the bigger that effect.

By contrast, SHY, which holds bonds maturing in 1 to 3 years, saw a price change of +1.9% over the same period, showing that the longer the maturity, the greater the risk of a price fall when rates rise. Using public market data as of November 21, 2026 and major US bond ETFs, this article sets out how rate changes affect bond prices and how drawdowns differ by maturity.

Why do bond ETF prices fall when rates rise?

When the benchmark market rate rises, the trading price of bonds already issued falls, and so does the price of ETFs that hold those bonds. Once newly issued bonds pay higher rates, demand to sell existing bonds with relatively low interest grows. Bond ETFs listed overseas and traded in dollars are affected not only by these rate changes but also by exchange rates.

Bond prices move in the opposite direction to market rates, and the longer the duration, the larger the maximum drawdown over the past 5 years. Comparing 5-year maximum drawdowns (MDD: the percentage fall from the highest value to the lowest value), TLT, which invests in very long-term bonds, recorded -50.04%. In contrast, IEF (iShares 7-10 Year Treasury Bond ETF), with intermediate maturities, had a 5-year maximum drawdown of -23.58%, and SHY (iShares 1-3 Year Treasury Bond ETF), which holds short-term bonds, only -6.23%.

So the longer the maturity, the larger the duration, and the more the price fell when rates rose. By the same principle, very long-term bond ETFs rose more in weeks when rates fell, and over a period when rates rose sharply, the 5-year maximum drawdown grew as large as -50.04%, as with TLT. When choosing between funds with different maturities, it is important to gauge in advance how large the price swings can be.

Why the size of the fall depends on maturity and duration

Over the past year, rates rose in 28 weeks and fell in 24, and each time the size of the price move differed with duration.

For every 0.1%p rise in US Treasury yields, TLT fell -1.25% on average, while SHY fell only -0.16%. IEF, with maturities of 7 to 10 years, responded with an average of -0.70% per 0.1%p rise. Looking only at weeks when rates rose, TLT moved -1.10% on average, IEF -0.58% and SHY -0.07%, keeping the same order of price sensitivity by maturity.

Conversely, in weeks when rates fell, TLT rose +1.10% on average, IEF +0.60% and SHY +0.16%, confirming that rates and bond prices move in opposite directions. The correlation coefficient (-1 to +1), which shows how closely two numbers move together, was -0.99 for IEF, -0.95 for TLT and -0.85 for SHY, all close to -1, showing a strong opposite relationship between rising rates and falling prices. These regression slopes are averages over the past year, and actual responses can differ as markets change.

Accounts and taxes for overseas-listed bond ETFs

When investing in overseas-listed bond ETFs such as TLT, IEF and SHY, which are listed in the US, the accounts you can use and the way they are taxed differ from Korea-listed products. Here is which accounts can hold them and how they are taxed.

Overseas-listed ETFs cannot be bought directly in Korea's ISA, pension savings or IRP accounts. To invest in US Treasuries with the tax benefits of those accounts, one way is to buy a Korea-listed bond ETF that tracks the same index. The tax difference between the two is covered in overseas-listed vs Korea-listed ETF taxes.

In Korea, trading gains on US-listed ETFs are subject to capital gains tax, which investors report and pay themselves in May of the following year. Dividends arrive after US tax has been deducted in advance (withholding), and the tax paid abroad is deducted when Korean tax is calculated (foreign tax credit).

🔸 Do US bond ETF prices rise sharply when rates fall?

Bond ETF prices generally rise when rates fall, but gains or losses in Korean won can differ with exchange rates. Overseas-listed ETFs trade in dollars, so even if bond prices rise after a rate cut, a fall in the dollar can lower the final return in won or turn it into a loss.

Summary

The difference among the three ETFs came down to one thing: how long their maturities are. Before guessing which way rates will move, what you can know first is how sensitive your chosen bond ETF is to the same change in rates.

In one line: the longer a bond ETF's maturity, the more it moved for the same change in rates, and its 5-year maximum drawdown followed the same order.
