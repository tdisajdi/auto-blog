The high distributions paid by covered call ETFs come not purely from stock dividends but from option premiums earned by selling the right to buy the underlying assets. As of November 21, 2026, the yield of US-listed JEPI was 8.41% and that of QYLD 13.43%, but this income comes in exchange for giving up share-price gains in rising markets. Compared with the 0.51% yield of QQQ, an ordinary stock ETF that simply follows its index, the numbers can be misleading: the trade-off is that price gains are capped and the price can fall.

Where exactly does the high distribution come from?

A covered call ETF uses the money it receives from selling call options on the assets it holds (the option premium) to fund its distributions (the money an ETF pays out to investors).

While the stock ETF QQQ yields around 0.51%, an ETF using a covered call strategy creates an extra source of income on top of stock dividends. While holding the underlying assets, it sells call options, the right to buy those assets at a set price, to a third party, and collects the option premium from the sale.

The option premium collected this way is the main source of the distributions paid to investors monthly or quarterly. That is why the distribution yield is far higher than the underlying assets' dividends (JEPI 8.41%, QYLD 13.43%).

What do you give up in exchange?

In exchange, you give up part of the gain when the underlying assets rise sharply, and even with high distributions, total return can be low if the price falls.

Because call options have been sold, even if the underlying price surges beyond a certain level, the ETF's price is held near that cap. When prices fall, the option premium cushions the fall only slightly, and the fund remains exposed to the decline in asset prices.

Looking at the maximum drawdown over the past 5 years (MDD, the percentage fall from the highest value to the lowest value), QYLD recorded -34.49% and JEPI -20.01% in dollar terms. QQQ's maximum drawdown over the same period was -35.54%, so QYLD fell about as much as QQQ while JEPI fell less. These drawdowns are in dollars, so exchange-rate changes are not included.

Price and distributions in actual returns

Over the past 3 years, QQQ's total return was +60.5%, while the high-yielding QYLD returned +35.0% and JEPI +26.9%.

Looking at price alone over the past year, JEPI fell -3.7% and QYLD -8.3%, while QQQ rose +1.4%. On a total-return basis with distributions reinvested, JEPI returned +4.3%, QYLD +3.4% and QQQ +1.9%. The share added by distributions over this period (%p, the difference between two rates) was largest for QYLD at +11.7%p, followed by JEPI at +8.0%p and QQQ at +0.5%p.

Over the longer 3-year period, on price alone QQQ rose +57.8% while JEPI managed only +0.1% and QYLD fell -6.4%. Distributions added +41.4%p for QYLD, +26.7%p for JEPI and +2.7%p for QQQ.

Meanwhile, total distributions over the past year compared with the year before rose 8.99% for QQQ and 5.62% for QYLD, but fell -3.31% for JEPI. This shows that the amount distributed can itself change from year to year with stock and options market conditions.

Frequently asked questions

🔸 How high are the distribution yields of US covered call ETFs?

The yield of US-listed covered call ETFs is about 8.41% for JEPI and 13.43% for QYLD. That is far higher than the 0.51% yield of QQQ, an ordinary index ETF, but it includes premiums from selling options.

🔸 Can Korean tax-advantaged accounts buy overseas-listed covered call ETFs directly?

ETFs listed overseas, such as in the US, cannot be bought directly in tax-advantaged accounts such as ISA, pension savings or IRP. To use those accounts' benefits, one option is a Korea-listed ETF that follows the same index or strategy.

Summary

A high distribution yield does not mean your money grows quickly. The limits of the covered call structure show when the underlying assets fall or rise strongly, and looking at total return, price change plus distributions, makes the difference clear (3 years: QQQ +60.5%, QYLD +35.0%, JEPI +26.9%).

There are three things you can check yourself when looking at covered call products.

1. How the price moved excluding distributions, such as the past year's price change (e.g., JEPI -3.7%, QYLD -8.3%, QQQ +1.4%)

2. How much the share added by distributions (e.g., past year: QYLD +11.7%p, JEPI +8.0%p) makes up for it in total return

3. Whether total distributions grew or shrank over the past year (e.g., QQQ 8.99%, QYLD 5.62%, JEPI -3.31%)

In one line: the high distributions of covered call ETFs are the price received for giving up part of the upside, and their 3-year total returns were lower than QQQ's.
