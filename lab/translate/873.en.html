What does each ETF invest in, and what are the fees?

VOO is a Vanguard fund listed on September 7, 2010 that tracks the Standard & Poor's 500 Index. QQQ, listed on March 10, 1999 and run by Invesco, uses the NASDAQ-100 Index as its benchmark. VOO's annual fee is 0.03%, and QQQ's is 0.18% a year.

Net assets, which show each fund's total size, are about $1.7357 trillion for VOO and about $463.8 billion for QQQ. The two differ in fees by 0.15 percentage points a year (a percentage point is the difference between two rates). Compared with a fund whose fee is 0.1 percentage points higher, keeping 100 million won invested for 10 years gives a cost difference of about 992,826 won for VOO. For QQQ, the same comparison with a fund 0.1 percentage points more expensive gives a difference of about 979,492 won over 10 years on 100 million won.

When comparing funds that cover the same index or a similar area, you can look at the size of net assets, i.e. the actual amount managed, as well as the stated fee.

How do dividends, taxes and currency differ?

On dividends (distributions, the money an ETF pays out to investors), the two funds differ in cash flow. The recent dividend yield (the past year's dividends divided by the current price) was 1.17% for VOO and 0.51% for QQQ.

On dividend tax, VOO's annual dividends are subject to 15% US withholding tax (tax deducted in advance when dividends or interest are paid). If you had put in 100 million won, a 1.17% dividend yield would mean annual dividends of about 1.17 million won with about 180,000 won of tax. For Korean residents, if total annual financial income such as interest and dividends exceeds 20 million won, the excess is combined with other income and taxed under comprehensive financial income taxation, and dividends from overseas ETFs count toward this threshold.

For capital gains, a Korean investor in overseas-listed funds deducts 2.5 million won a year from total gains, applies a 22% rate and files a return directly in May of the following year. With a gain of 10 million won in a year, capital gains tax comes to about 1.65 million won after the 2.5 million won deduction and the 22% rate. Both funds trade in dollars, so they carry currency exposure (exchange rate changes flow straight into returns). Overseas-listed ETFs cannot be bought directly in Korean ISA, pension savings or IRP accounts; the tax benefits of those accounts are available through Korea-listed ETFs that track the same index.

Which features fit which situation?

On the ability to bear losses (volatility), the two funds' actual drawdowns give a clear yardstick. Applying the actual five-year maximum drawdown to 100 million won, VOO could have lost up to 25.41 million won, leaving 74.59 million won, while QQQ could have lost 35.54 million won, leaving 64.46 million won.

On dividend growth, the past year's total dividends rose 5.57% for VOO and 8.99% for QQQ. Quarter to quarter, however, there is a difference in how steady the payments are. VOO's payment fell from the previous one in 1 of its last 4 quarterly dividends (1.8230 USD paid on September 28, 2026, -7.08% from the previous payment). QQQ's payment fell from the previous one in 2 of its last 4 (0.7510 USD on September 21, 2026, -7.63%, and 0.7330 USD on March 23, 2026, -7.68%).

If a fall of 35% or more in a market slump is hard to accept, or if you want steadier quarterly dividends, VOO's lower MDD fits better. If you can accept a drawdown more than 10 percentage points deeper and want a portfolio that follows the Nasdaq-100 index with relatively higher one-year dividend growth (8.99%), QQQ's features fit better.

FAQ

🔸 Do dividends from overseas-listed ETFs count toward comprehensive financial income taxation in Korea?

Yes. Dividends from overseas-listed ETFs are added to interest and dividend income earned in Korea and abroad, and if the total exceeds 20 million won a year, they fall under comprehensive financial income taxation. Gains from selling overseas-listed ETFs (capital gains), however, are taxed separately, so they do not count toward the 20 million won threshold.

Summary

Comparing only short-term returns when looking at overseas-listed index funds makes it easy to overlook the downside risk that shows up in falling markets. VOO and QQQ had similar average annual returns over five years, but differed widely in the maximum drawdown you would have had to sit through.

When you next look at similar index funds or their published data, you can use three yardsticks. First, how much total cost builds up over a long holding period when annual fees differ by 0.1 percentage points or more. Second, apply the maximum drawdown (MDD) of the past five years to your own principal to work out the loss you could actually bear (remaining assets versus principal). Third, alongside the dividend yield, count how many recent quarterly dividends fell from the previous payment to see how variable the cash flow is.

In one line: it is worth looking at drawdowns and fees as well as long-term returns: the two ETFs had similar five-year averages (9.6% and 9.4%), but their maximum drawdowns were -25.41% and -35.54%.
